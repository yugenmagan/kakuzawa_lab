Lingering attachment and resignation

I would like to explore a little further the emotional dimension discussed in my previous essay (“Impressions and Reflections / A Tragic Love Story”).

“Lingering attachment”

“How long are you going to lie there, clinging to sleep?!” Yuka says while kicking Kouta. Here miren is an everyday expression. But Lucy's line in volume four, “I can't bring myself to die because I still have something I can't let go of…”, startled me.

Not because I thought a child could not know that word. Rather, Lucy uses “lingering attachment” in a sense one might encounter in classical literature.

She is asking Kouta to kill her, so death itself is not what frightens her. What she cannot relinquish is not some vague attachment to life, but something specific: a “faint hope” (why faint will be discussed next). In short: “With Kouta…”

The contents of her letter, revealed later — “I'd like to make it into a wedding ring…” — substantiate that faint hope.

Therefore, if Kouta kills her — indeed, only if Kouta himself kills her — nothing will remain to bind her. It is an unbearably poignant and weighty line.

“Resignation”

To give a mundane example, lotteries come in many forms, all with laughably small odds. Most buyers think, “I know I'll lose, but wouldn't it be wonderful if I won?”

Lucy extends this attitude even to happiness that seems almost within reach. Marriage aside, surely she could at least ask to be held…

She doesn't even seem to think, “What if he dislikes me?” That may make her look especially pessimistic, but I think this is not pessimism; it is resignation (teikan).

A pessimist can still advance, however fearfully. A resigned person sits down on the spot, bereft of the will to try. In a way resignation is much worse.

When one is young and the future bright, resignation scarcely enters one's mind. Growing older means confronting realities beyond one's control and suffering setbacks. Minor setbacks are manageable, but major ones can cultivate resignation.

Lucy's setbacks are portrayed through her circumstances and bullying. Yet are these enough to account for such profound resignation? I doubt it.

What follows is pure, and perhaps impertinent, speculation: could Lucy's resignation be less a deliberate literary construction than a projection of the creator's own view of life?

According to the biography on his website, he once gave up his dream. The job he subsequently took does not strike me as one chosen merely on a whim. Later he returned to his dream. Yet the distance between office employee and professional manga artist cannot have been crossed in a single stride, even with prior experience.

Perhaps this experience gave the creator a sense of resignation. (I know how discourteous this speculation is, yet I could not leave it unsaid. Please forgive me.)

A human being of flesh and blood

Historical heroes and shōnen protagonists forge ahead with unwavering convictions, but naturally that is embellishment. Real people hesitate, agonize and doubt.

The coexistence of two contradictory impulses — resignation and lingering attachment — makes Lucy feel human.

Resigned, Lucy gives up from the outset. By killing Kouta's father and sister, she believes she has severed her lingering ties. Yet in volume twelve, atop the lighthouse, she cries in anguish: “Even so, I really am lonely all by myself.”

Her cry in the face of life's helplessness moves me profoundly.

Projection

“Where shall I seek the shrine of the Chancellor? Beyond Brocade City, in a dense grove of cypresses. Green grass by the steps welcomes spring on its own; behind the leaves an oriole sings its lovely song in vain. Thrice summoned to plan the fate of the realm, the old minister served two reigns with all his heart. Before his campaign could triumph he died; ever since, heroes have soaked their robes with tears.”

This is Du Fu's poem “The Chancellor of Shu.” I am in no position to offer a scholarly commentary on Chinese poetry. What concerns me is Du Fu's state of mind.

In brief, the poem mourns the tragic death of a hero of antiquity. But it is not mere pity of the kind we feel when television shows whales or dolphins stranded on a beach.

For thousands of years, educated Chinese regarded participation in government as their highest calling, while farming or commerce was considered lowly. Du Fu too aspired to public office, but his excessive straightforwardness thwarted his ambitions.

He was forty-nine at the time. Life was hard, and by the life expectancy of his age he could already glimpse its end. Still he could not abandon his ambition to become a statesman: his “lingering attachment,” his “faint hope.”

He compared Zhuge Liang's great accomplishments with his own misfortune, while seeing in the hero's untimely death a reflection of his own possible fate. Thus arose a boundless, inexpressible emotion that brought unending tears.

The essence

The preceding section has turned into something like a school lesson. Yet I believe the true appeal of this work differs greatly depending on whether one understands Du Fu's feelings. That is why I brought him up.

It is presumptuous of me to say so, but if you do not understand, keep your copy rather than selling it. When life deals you a “great setback,” please read it again.

From Lucy's first meeting with Kouta to the ending, where “attachment” and “resignation” are transcended through reincarnation into a happiness that is not perfect but “good enough,” I think you will feel an immeasurable depth of emotion.

I believe this work contains something far more precious than much recent literature preoccupied with wordplay.

“What an exaggeration!”

I sound entirely like an old man. Well, I am one…
