Subtle Differences Between the Anime and Manga Endings

Wayback Machine — original archive

Anime Kouta and the Kouta of the Original Manga

When the anime first ended, the greatest controversy seems to have concerned Kouta's attitude toward Lucy. Lucy had killed his father and his younger sister Kanae. Yet although he said he could not forgive her, he still wished for her to live. That moving conclusion was one reason the anime Elfen Lied came to be regarded as a minor masterpiece—“gory, but moving enough to make you cry.” Nevertheless, the scene is undeniably forced, and quite a few viewers found Kouta's conclusion itself difficult to accept.

That made me especially curious about the manga's ending. When Kouta said in EL84 that he had to avenge his father and Kanae, I wondered whether the manga's Kouta would reach a different conclusion. In the end, though, he seems to have arrived at much the same answer: “I cannot forgive you,” yet he still wanted Lucy to survive.

Why did Kouta want Lucy to live after she had killed his family? I see three points common to both versions. First, once his childhood memories returned, Kouta remembered that he had genuinely shared some happy moments playing with Lucy's personality, however brief. Second, alongside his hatred for Lucy, he loved the Nyu personality. Third, Kanae's death contained the potential to push him either toward accepting Lucy or rejecting her.

Let me elaborate on the third point. Deep down, I think Kouta considered himself partly responsible for Kanae's death and developed an almost compulsive need to save any girl whom nobody understood. If saving lonely, misunderstood girls was his way of atoning to Kanae, there is a bitter irony in the fact that the most obvious example was Lucy—the very person who killed Kanae. In the anime, a conversation between Mayu and Yuka hints at this; in the manga Kouta refers directly to Kanae.

Even so, there is some distance between the anime and manga in Kouta's feelings for Lucy.

In the anime, Lucy and Kouta kiss. LILIUM plays, and memories of their past return in vivid flashes. Forced though it is, this is one of the anime's finest scenes. On my second viewing I cried uncontrollably (oddly enough, not on the first). I think the anime's Lucy did become Kouta's lover, even if only for a moment—the relationship she had longed for. In the manga, by contrast, she never attains that relationship. If we take Kouta's words literally, they remain friends.

Even if those twins are Lucy and Nyu, then yes, they can “stay close to him.” But is that what Lucy truly wanted most? I am not so sure.

In the original, Lucy's greatest wish is never fulfilled. At the final threshold, I cannot help sensing a quietly resigned philosophy of life.

▲ ▼ How closely do the anime's themes match the original?

Why did these differences arise? I cannot help thinking that the creators' conceptions of the work differed subtly.

Director Kanbe defined the anime's themes as “discrimination and salvation.” On discrimination, he wrote about bullying as a social problem: a social perspective. In volume 10, page 210, Okamoto-sensei has Director Kakuzawa say that “discrimination is rooted deep in humanity,” citing skin color and differences between “species”: a biological perspective. Different viewpoints, but discrimination clearly belongs to both works.

What about the other theme, “salvation”? This is perhaps more interesting.

The anime's orientation toward salvation is evident not only from the director's remarks. A producer also wrote, in effect: “We are all seriously thinking about how to make everyone at Maple House happy! We rack our brains over it through sleepless nights!!!” The staff apparently shared the aim of giving the story an ending with some form of salvation.

The structure of the anime supports this reading. Director Kakuzawa's dialogue is drastically cut, while as much time as possible is reserved for Kouta and Lucy's childhood. The anime even introduces foreshadowing absent from the manga: the music box and the stopped clock. It is easy to imagine that the script was designed to impress their shared past upon the audience and prepare the way for salvation.

And the original? I believe the manga, too, is oriented toward salvation. Consider Mayu and Nana, accepted into Maple House; Bando, whose loneliness is eased by Mayu; and Lucy, whose acceptance or rejection by Kouta becomes a key issue.

Yet salvation in the manga seems to rest on an extremely precarious balance. Why, for instance, does Mariko's arc in volume 7 sustain such extraordinary tension? Is it merely because the story is unpredictable? I think not. Mariko is cruel, but hers is a soul that deserves salvation. Will she, as Shirakawa puts it, “recover her human heart”? The story advances as though neither the readers nor perhaps even the author can see the answer. Again and again in Elfen Lied, lonely characters who desperately crave love seem on the verge of rescue—only to have the ladder pulled away.

▲ ▼ The wavering characters of the original

I believe the narrative focus of Elfen Lied has a certain instability.

The childhood arc depicts the rupture between Lucy and Kouta. From Kouta's “Long time no see” in volume 10, page 34, right through the final volume, the question is whether they can reconcile. The balance was so delicate that many readers were certain Kouta would reject Lucy. I, too, watched anxiously to see which way things would turn.

In volumes 8 and 9, another character's feelings were wavering: Nana. She constantly agonized over whether to kill Nyu/Lucy. Bando's death made her resolve to fight, but Mayu's words plunged her back into doubt. She was torn between her affection for Nyu and her promise to Papa.

In volumes 6 and 7, the focus was whether Kurama and Mariko's shattered parent-child relationship could be restored.

Seen together, these arcs form an ongoing tug-of-war: will the relationship between two central characters break apart or be renewed?

▲ ▼ A curious choice of focus

Stories about broken and restored relationships are hardly rare. What strikes me as unusual is that Elfen Lied's setting would readily support entirely different narrative focuses.

One characteristic of the series is the parallel development of its science-fiction Institute storyline and the romantic comedy at Maple House. The Institute, for instance, could have been used to turn the manga into a Shonen Jump-style battle series: “Unless the evil queen bee Lucy is defeated, humanity will perish!!! Assassins attack Lucy one after another. Can she finally be defeated?” Nana was, after all, introduced in precisely that manner. Yet the author did not pursue it.

Alternatively, the romantic-comedy premise could have been developed into the central plot: “Whom will Kouta choose—Lucy or Yuka?” The story uses that possibility around EL50–51 in volume 6, when Yuka decides to leave Maple House, but it never becomes the central question of the series.

Instead of exploiting these appealing premises, the story focuses on “loneliness” and “longing for love.” Given the setting, this seems strange. Whenever I read Elfen Lied, I find myself wondering whether the battle and romance premises went underused because the author's own preoccupations drew the work in another direction. To put it more pointedly: “Could the writer himself have spent a long time agonizing over whether he would be accepted for who he is?”

Rereading with this possibility in mind, Mayu repeatedly remembers not so much the sexual abuse as her mother's words, “It would be fine if you disappeared from this world” (volume 3, page 17). What hurts her most is losing Wanta and being left entirely alone. Nana and Lucy, too, are intensely concerned with whether there is a place for them to belong.

▲ ▼ The subtle difference between anime and manga: salvation on a knife-edge

Both versions seek to ease their protagonists' loneliness and restore lost bonds. But while salvation seems to have been assured in the anime from the beginning, the manga may reflect the creator's own uncertainties. Some form of catastrophe was always possible. That, I think, is the difference.

This also helps explain why the manga's ending (particularly the collected-volume version) met with considerable opposition. Judging by blogs, many readers broadly welcomed its redemptive ending, but the proportion of those unconvinced was far from negligible.

I personally received the collected-volume ending positively, but consider the existence of dissent extremely important. Certainly some developments, such as Bando's survival, were difficult to believe. But that was not the only issue. Some readers may not have wanted the characters saved at all; perhaps an ending in which humanity became extinct would have seemed more like Elfen Lied. Was this not a story built up through relentless coldness, in which even the most important characters died when their time came?

The very fact that some readers wanted salvation while others wanted destruction seems to prove how narrowly the story tipped toward salvation. Lucy's partial redemption, without obtaining the thing she wanted most, seems another expression of that precarious balance.
