Wayback Machine · Japanese original

Elfen Lied and Devilman

Works often compared with this one When I first encountered Elfen Lied, the work it reminded me of most was Devilman. Many others have noticed the resemblance. Along with Neon Genesis Evangelion, Devilman is probably the title most often mentioned in blogs as a similar work.

Scenes that invite comparison When people mention Devilman, they almost invariably point to Lucy’s line, “You are the ones who aren’t human” (volume 4, page 89). Near the end of the Devilman manga there is a horrific human hunt. People grow suspicious of one another and, like witch-hunters, persecute “undesirables” who might turn into demons. The protagonist’s girlfriend and her parents are killed. Akira Fudo cries out: “You are the demons! My body became that of a demon, but I never lost my human heart! You have human bodies, yet you have become demons! So this is what the people I was prepared to sacrifice my life to protect really are! Go to hell, humans!” He then kills the hunters. Lucy’s words during her killings are strikingly similar. Yet on closer inspection the works are not simply different: their themes seem to point in opposite directions.

Devilman’s view of humanity In Devilman, the hero’s killing of humans and rejection of humanity as a species occur very near the end. This is a climax. Humanity then perishes astonishingly easily—in only a few lines of text! Although the demons tried to corrupt mankind, the outcome also releases the hideous madness already present in people. Humans are ultimately portrayed as innately evil and insane: upbringing may restrain them, but some trigger can make them delight in slaughtering and dismembering one another. The hero’s girlfriend is torn apart and beheaded by her neighbors, who seem almost to enjoy themselves. Akira, who had struggled to defend humanity, therefore becomes indifferent to its fate. Perhaps the author also no longer saw any reason to depict it. The finale belongs entirely to the Devilman army and the demons.

Elfen Lied’s view of humanity Lucy likewise kills people and at one point rejects humanity as a species. But what was Devilman’s conclusion becomes the beginning of change in Elfen Lied. Devilman stresses innate madness; Elfen Lied acknowledges innate evil but attaches great importance to upbringing and environment. Lucy is a Diclonius, and her DNA’s will may demand humanity’s destruction; nevertheless, had her dog not been killed, perhaps she would never have become a murderer. She later meets Kouta. Childhood experiences taught her to hate humanity, but she also experienced first love; killing Kanae and witnessing Kouta’s anguish awakened guilt. While reading I repeatedly thought of Schopenhauer’s idea of compassion—sharing another’s suffering as a basis of ethics. By feeling Kouta’s pain, Lucy begins to recover her humanity. Rather than an ending, this is the beginning of redemption. Thus Elfen Lied may even be an antithesis to Devilman despite approaching a remarkably similar subject.

What Devilman and Elfen Lied share If a later work consciously sets out to challenge an earlier one, it would probably keep their other themes as close as possible. There are indeed many similarities. The Diclonius expose the divide between humanity and another species—Homo sapiens and, in the story’s terms, Homo diclonius. The old-versus-new humanity pattern resembles the humans and the Devilman army: in both, a new kind of humanity arises within the old. Both works focus less on states and political systems than on the human species itself. Okamoto has repeatedly mentioned his fondness for Gundam, but there questions about humanity are inseparable from political structures, nations, and war, making the resemblance weaker. I feel similarly about Ideon. Elfen Lied, meanwhile, pays far more attention to intensely personal troubles, something less prominent in Devilman. In that respect it also resembles Evangelion; I hope to write about this separately someday. It may relate to Elfen Lied’s growing classification as a sekai-kei work. What strikes me most, however, is how directly both portray madness hidden within human beings. Both show how cruel humans can become. Both are notorious for extreme violence closely tied to their central themes. To me this is their greatest similarity.

Elfen Lied as an antithesis to Devilman As I said, the protagonist’s killing of people and rejection of humanity are Devilman’s ending but the beginning of redemption in Elfen Lied. One emphasizes innate madness, the other acquired experience. I do not know whether this image communicates my thought: if Devilman descended into the human soul and found savage madness, Elfen Lied seems to answer, “Yes, it exists—but it is not the whole story.” If Devilman concludes, “Humans harbor cruel madness and therefore do not deserve to exist; let them perish,” Elfen Lied concludes, “Humans harbor cruel madness, but they are more than that; therefore they should not perish.” I think this because so many characters introduced as outright villains reveal another side before leaving the story. Mariko is initially described as ready to kill everyone in sight (EL52), even called “the worst Silpelit” in the anime (episode 10), but ultimately regains the parental love she craved. Through Mayu, Bando realizes he wanted someone who would cry for him, and temporarily exits the story. Nousou initially feels no guilt about extracting Diclonius spinal cords, yet apologizes to Barbara—“I’m sorry”—before his head is torn off. Chief Kakuzawa seems the principal villain, but even he shows fatherly concern for his daughter in extra volume pages. Both stories could have ended with humanity’s extinction. This is precisely why I believed Elfen Lied would not. The author depicts human cruelty without flinching, yet seems to ask, “Is cruelty really all there is to us?” He also depicts longing for affection, loneliness, remorse, and love between parents and children. I found it difficult to believe he would condemn humanity as a whole. Even so, I was terribly anxious…

What did Elfen Lied have that Devilman did not? After the serialization ended much as I expected, I spent a long time wondering how Elfen Lied had become an antithesis to Devilman. One day an idea occurred to me that initially seemed far-fetched even to myself. Almost a year later I still wonder, “Could it be?” I will venture to put it here. What follows is something I wrote on October 12, 2005, when, for certain reasons, I was writing as hard as I could for Lucy’s sake.

“This is probably my last message. One last thing I want to mention is the concrete difference from Devilman. What I cannot avoid thinking about is the very title of this tournament: moe. Yes, moe. The anime concerns Lucy’s personal discrimination, atonement, and salvation, without going too far into the manga’s themes of human nature and coexistence between species. Yet I still think it asks what it means to be human. Akira Fudo desperately tries to protect people, only to despair of them and decide they are unworthy of protection. In Elfen Lied I think the opposite happens. Lucy takes an action resembling suicide and rejects becoming the mother of a new humanity. At the very end she protects the human world. Why this difference when the themes are so similar? One answer I keep coming back to is ‘moe.’ Perhaps that sounds rather deflating… If moe means being moved by every little action of a character, isn’t it natural that a story which follows its characters’ inner lives so carefully, however deeply it probes human darkness, does not condemn human existence itself as evil? People often call Elfen Lied’s approach manipulative. But if that very quality helped resist the Devilman worldview—a despairing view of humanity—then moe really was necessary. It makes for a strange and fascinating story. Perhaps this is only my own fancy. In any event, it is an extraordinarily strange, compelling, profound, and moving story. May as many people as possible become interested in it… That is all.”

The almost prayerful feelings of one person I would like to think separately about moe in Elfen Lied someday, but let me turn to something else. As I watched the story, at some point my feelings became almost like a prayer. Nana was the first character I loved. Her willingness to give her life for Kurama moved me; the ending of anime episode four shocked me as if I had been pushed off a cliff. Near the end, when anyone could die at any moment, I watched almost praying that she would survive. “There are so many wonderful things… so many…” Why did those ordinary words strike me so powerfully? It was strange. At first I hated Lucy. But the flashback chapters turned my feelings instantly to sympathy. During the reconciliation between Kouta and Lucy in the anime’s final episode, I inexplicably cried without stopping when I watched it a second time. That emotion lasted for months and almost interfered with my work. So throughout the manga’s ending I continued to hope for their reconciliation. I love Nana, Mariko, Yuka—all of them. Before I knew it, I had become overwhelmingly attached to the characters. I never felt this when reading Devilman. Perhaps I fell perfectly into the creators’ hands. But loving the characters means, in a sense, properly seeing the appealing side of humanity, however cruel a character may be. If being moved by their every action may be called moe, then I again believe moe was essential to this work.
