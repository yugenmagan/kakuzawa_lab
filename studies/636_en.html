A Tragic Love Story

A Tragic Love Story

I would like to discuss the original Elfen Lied manga. First, though, let me tell the Legend of the White Snake, which some readers may know.

The Legend of the White Snake

Two snakes, white and green, over a thousand years old, lived on Mount Emei. Longing for human life, they descended as beautiful women called Bai Suzhen and Xiaoqing. While sheltering from rain they met a young man, Xu Xian. Bai fell in love at first sight, pursued him the very next day, and married him. She was a wonderful wife, like the heroine of Oh My Goddess!

One day the monk Fahai came from Jinshan Temple and told Xu Xian, “Your wife is a snake.” Xu denied it. But Fahai tricked him into discovering her true form, and the shock nearly killed him. Risking her life, Bai went to collect the miraculous resurrection herb lingzhi. (I will skip the battle here.) Xu revived, Bai managed to conceal her identity, and they reconciled.

Fahai returned, insisting that humans must not live with spirits, and lured Xu to Jinshan to make him a monk. Bai and Xiaoqing begged for his release; Fahai refused. They fought, but pregnant Bai could not use her full power and was driven away. At Broken Bridge, where the couple first met, they reunited with Xu, who had escaped. Xiaoqing demanded, “Which matters more, my elder sister or that damned monk?” She even tried to kill him. Bai stopped her and confessed, “I really am a snake, but I love you all the same.” Xu repented: “I don't care what you are. Let us live together.” They reunited and had a child. Happiness seemed assured.

Then Fahai attacked a third time. Ignoring Xu's pleas to stop, he summoned Buddhist guardian deities and sealed Bai and Xiaoqing inside Leifeng Pagoda. The story apparently derives from a tale in Stories to Caution the World (1624), and has many variants, including happier endings for readers who found this conclusion unbearable.

The mechanism of tragic love

Most people sympathize with Bai and resent Fahai's injustice. His stated justification is that spirits kill humans, but Bai clearly does not. Discovering that one's beloved is a snake would be a shock, yet Bai's goddess-like goodness and Fahai's self-righteousness neutralize it. The “snake,” “goddess,” and “antagonist” together produce tragic love.

The snake is a necessary shackle on the heroine. Xiaoqing is a snake too and is sealed alongside her, yet she could just as easily have been human: her snake nature matters only in her role as Bai's younger companion. Thus in Peking opera she can apparently become an especially charming, in modern terms “moe,” character. Does that not resemble Nana in Elfen Lied?

Bai was originally a vicious monster, and only later became the heroine we know. Perhaps otherwise Fahai would have been justified and the tale would have remained a straightforward monster-slaying story rather than tragic love.

And in Elfen Lied…

“Nyu-nyu-nyu?!” Lucy, the heroine, behaves like a demon. Neither Nyu's existence nor Kurama's field team, playing Fahai's part, can sufficiently counterbalance her brutality. The obvious conclusion would be simply “Lucy must die.” We need an injustice greater than Lucy's savage fighting.

“Exterminate old humanity, create a world of Diclonius, and become a god!!!” EXCELLENT! Now that is outrageously unreasonable! It greatly improves the balance. Yet Lucy's poison remains potent, so along comes “DNA.” Viewers may now think, “Poor Lucy.” All Diclonius possess this DNA, but Nana, even when apparently listening to it, does not become a savage killer. Mariko and her clones die as human beings. Lucy's DNA is incomparably stronger: in the end it even takes possession of her body.

Bai's shackle was being a snake. Lucy's is less her Diclonius nature than this exceptionally powerful original DNA. It both restrains her and softens our judgment of her. That makes her pitiful indeed.

Reason and emotion

I have some doubts about comparisons with Devilman and Evangelion. Their themes seem extremely intellectual, perhaps overintellectualized, as though they willingly enter dead ends. Western thought's endless “Why?” has driven modern science, but sometimes resembles trying to lift yourself by your own collar.

I digress. Certainly the depictions and settings have similarities, but are their themes alike? I think not. SEELE is central to Evangelion's theme and remains active to the end. (I take Eva's theme to be “Stay, thou art so fair!”—the reverse of impermanence—with Instrumentality its concrete program.)

By contrast, Director Kakuzawa, who drives the Diclonius premise, exits the stage in a ridiculous fashion. The final battle is not humans versus Diclonius but “Lucy and Nyu versus DNA.” If the theme were the intellectual issues of coexistence and discrimination, this would seem like an abandoned plot.

Lucy, Nana, and Mariko suffer above all from being alone, not merely from being Diclonius. Is Diclonius identity a device for making them lonely? Almost all the human characters are lonely too. This is rooted not in reason but in emotion.

Considering the ending

The manga's great distinction is a vicious heroine who shatters the usual tragic-romance mold. Devilman-like depictions may simply make her viciousness vivid. As the White Snake comparison suggests, tragic romance balances the audience's emotions through a delicate arithmetic.

If reason is paramount, Lucy's reincarnation at the end may seem intolerable. But total disappearance would turn tragic love into pure tragedy. (Bai is imprisoned, not annihilated; some versions rescue her.) Kandata in Ryunosuke Akutagawa's The Spider's Thread commits countless evils and goes to hell, yet because he once spared a spider a thread of salvation descends. Too generous? This is an Eastern kind of salvation. Killing absolutely everyone and throwing away all that has been built up would go beyond tragedy into comedy.

Many readers apparently take the twins at the end to be Diclonius. It depends on whether this is “the story of the Diclonius” or “the story of Lucy alone.” I emphatically choose the latter. If the twins are Diclonius, that seems more punishment than salvation. (Forgive my harshness toward Nana; recall what I said about Xiaoqing.) The ribbon functions like a Wagnerian leitmotif. And the curtain falls on “With friends…,” remaining true to tragic love to the end. How sad!

In conclusion

People seeking reason in fiction may dismiss tragic love, emotion, and Eastern ideas as trite or small. I still think this is a universal theme. That is why the White Snake story—and, though they are not tragic romances, Romance of the Three Kingdoms and Chushingura—remain beloved.

I want to give this work a huge circled gold star. I have sounded pompous and categorical, but, as the character might say, “Well, anything goes if it's interesting!” I'm a newcomer who wrote this because I was so moved. I hope it helps pass the time.
